Gunite and Shotcrete Deficiencies in Swimming Pool Construction-Defect Litigation

By Randy Beard · Swimming pool & construction defect expert witness · Updated November 7, 2026

When a pool cracks, leaks, or fails prematurely, the investigation usually begins at the shell. For the vast majority of California in-ground pools, that shell is built from gunite (dry-process shotcrete) or wet-mix shotcrete — a pneumatically applied concrete that forms the structural backbone of the entire pool. When this material is applied incorrectly, the defects can be invisible to the naked eye for years, surfacing only after significant damage has already occurred.

As a swimming pool expert witness with 47 years in the industry, I have inspected many pools with shell failures traceable to deficient shotcrete or gunite application. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for construction-defect attorneys evaluating pool claims in California.

What the Standards Require

The governing technical standard for shotcrete application is ACI 506R-16 (Guide to Shotcrete), published by the American Concrete Institute, along with the companion specification document ACI 506.2-13. For residential pools, ANSI/APSP/ICC-5 establishes minimum structural design requirements, including minimum shell wall and floor thickness (generally 3.5 inches, with some areas requiring more depending on load and depth).

ACI 318-19 (the structural concrete building code) governs reinforcing steel, including the critical requirement that concrete in contact with water or soil must provide a minimum 3 inches of cover over all rebar. This is one of the most commonly deficient dimensions I measure in defective pools.

Compressive strength is equally important. Per ACI 318, Table 19.3.3, concrete in contact with water must achieve a minimum of 4,000 psi at 28 days — a specification routinely missed when mix water ratios are not controlled in the field or when rebound material is inadvertently incorporated into the shell.

The Rebound Problem: The Most Prevalent Deficiency

Shotcrete application involves projecting a high-velocity stream of cement, aggregate, and water at the excavation walls and floor. Inevitably, some material bounces back — this is called rebound. Per ACI 506R-16, §7.5, rebound must be continuously removed from the work area before fresh shotcrete is applied over it.

When it isn't removed — a shortcut taken under time and cost pressure — the rebound becomes trapped inside the shell. The resulting zones consist largely of aggregate with little or no cement paste binding it together: essentially unbonded gravel embedded in an otherwise solid shell.

In litigation, these zones are identified through core sampling under ASTM C1604 (3-inch diameter minimum cores drilled from the pool shell) and petrographic examination under ASTM C856, which reveals the particle-to-paste ratio, void structure, and bonding quality under a microscope. When cores show high-rebound zones, you typically see:

  • Reduced or near-zero compressive strength at those locations
  • Honeycombing and internal voids
  • Pathways for water infiltration toward rebar
  • Rebar corrosion and subsequent spalling

Cold Joints: A Structural Plane of Weakness

Gunite and shotcrete applications on large pools are sometimes interrupted — end of shift, material supply delays, equipment failure. When the partially applied shell is allowed to set fully before application resumes, a cold joint forms at the interface. If the set surface is not cleaned, roughened, and pre-moistened before new material is applied, the bond across the joint is minimal.

Cold joints are structural defects. They represent a plane of negligible tensile or shear strength within the shell, and they are common initiation sites for the cracks that first alert homeowners to a problem. In a pool subject to soil movement, seismic activity, or hydrostatic pressure, a cold joint in the wrong location can lead to a full shell breach.

Rebar Cover Deficiencies and Corrosion

The three-inch minimum cover requirement exists for a straightforward reason: chlorinated, aerated pool water is corrosive to steel. Insufficient concrete cover over rebar allows water to migrate through the shell and reach the reinforcing steel, initiating corrosion. As iron oxides form, they expand — roughly 2.5 to 3 times the volume of the parent steel — generating internal tensile pressure that fractures the concrete cover from the inside out, a process called spalling.

In my inspections, cover deficiencies most commonly occur at corners, near fittings and penetrations, and in areas where rebar was improperly supported before application. The nozzle operator may not have been able to encapsulate the steel fully, or the rebar may have moved during shooting. Core sampling and non-destructive ground-penetrating radar (GPR) can locate these deficiencies without demolition.

Nozzle Technique and Sand Lenses

ACI 506R-16 specifies that the nozzle be held roughly perpendicular to the receiving surface at a distance of approximately 3 to 5 feet. Oblique angles produce uneven material distribution, with excess rebound on the proximal side and reduced compressive density on the distal side.

In dry-process (gunite) applications specifically, interrupted application can create sand lenses — thin laminar planes within the shell where aggregate concentration is high and cement paste is nearly absent. These planes have minimal tensile strength and are particularly prone to shear failure when the shell is subject to cyclic loading from soil pressure or temperature changes.

Evaluating These Defects in Litigation

California construction-defect claims involving pool shell failures typically proceed under two frameworks, depending on when the pool was built and sold:

  • SB 800 (Civil Code §895–945.5): Applies to new residential construction sold after January 1, 2003. Section 896(g)(2) specifically requires pools and spas not to crack due to construction or soil defects; §896(g)(1) requires them not to leak. SB 800 mandates a pre-litigation notice-and-repair process.
  • CCP §337.15: For pools outside SB 800's scope (commercial, older residential, post-sale additions), the 10-year absolute statute of limitations on latent construction defects governs — measured from substantial completion.

In either context, establishing a shotcrete deficiency requires physical evidence: cores, petrographic analysis, and, critically, the testimony of a qualified expert who can connect the observed defects to specific deviations from the applicable ACI, ANSI/APSP, and project specification standards.
