Vanishing-Edge Pool Failures: Where the Defects Hide and How They Are Proven

By Randy Beard · Swimming pool & construction defect expert witness · Updated November 7, 2026

A vanishing-edge pool, also called an infinity or negative-edge pool, is one of the hardest things a pool contractor builds. It is also one of the most common sources of high-dollar pool claims. The owner paid for water that seems to drop off into the view. When the edge runs unevenly, the catch basin overflows or runs dry, or the pool keeps losing water, the owner usually ends up in a construction defect dispute.

I have been in the pool industry for 47 years. I hold California C-27 and C-53 licenses and was formerly an investigator for the Contractors State License Board. As a swimming pool expert witness, I find that most vanishing-edge failures come from four sources: the edge itself, the catch basin, the hydraulics that link them, and the structure under the wall. This article covers each one and the evidence that tends to decide the case.

No Code Sets the Design, So the Standard of Care Does

Attorneys are often surprised to learn that no building code tells a contractor how big a residential catch basin must be or how much water must flow over the edge. The model codes and the ANSI/APSP/ICC standards cover structure, electrical, barriers, and suction entrapment. The hydraulic design of the edge is left to the designer.

That makes these cases depend on the standard of care. The question is whether the design and construction met established industry practice. That practice is well documented, so the work can be measured against published methods rather than opinion. One code requirement does apply directly. The suction-entrapment standard, now ANSI/PHTA/ICC-7, covers catch basins by name, so the suction outlets in the basin are part of the safety analysis, not just the hydraulics.

The Edge: Small Tolerances, Visible Results

A vanishing edge is a weir, and a weir only works if it is level. Water follows the low spots. If the edge is out of level, water sheets over one section and the rest goes dry, which is the most common complaint in these cases. Experienced builders work to about one-sixteenth of an inch along the full length of the edge.

When a contractor misses that tolerance, the usual fix is to turn up the pump so the whole edge is wet. That covers the symptom but not the defect. It costs the owner energy every month, adds turbulence and noise, and still leaves an uneven sheet when the system runs at normal flow.

The edge can be measured. A level survey of the weir, photographs of the water sheet at the design flow rate, and a review of the approved edge detail usually show whether the problem is in the construction or in how the system is being operated.

The Catch Basin: Surge Capacity and Water in Transit

The catch basin (or a remote surge tank) has to hold two volumes of water. The first is the water in transit, the layer flowing over the edge during normal operation. The second is surge, the water that bathers push over the edge. When the pump stops, the water in transit drains back into the basin. If the basin cannot hold it, the basin overflows.

The industry method is to size the basin for at least two inches of water over the whole pool surface, and more for small pools that see heavy use. For example, an 800-square-foot pool running a quarter inch over the edge has about 125 gallons in transit at any moment. Two inches over the same surface is about 1,000 gallons. A basin built to look good, not to hold that volume, will fail one way or the other.

Common basin defects include:

  • Undersized volume, which causes overflow when the pump stops and starves the pump during heavy use
  • Operating level and overflow set wrong, so surge drains straight out the overflow and the basin empties before the pool can refill to the edge
  • Auto-fill problems, from fill devices that are unreliable or set to the wrong level
  • No independent edge circulation, where suction lines shared with the main pool system can drain the pool if a check valve fails

A misread basin can look like a leak. An owner who sees the auto-fill running constantly may assume the shell is cracked when the water is actually leaving through a badly set overflow. Telling these apart takes water-loss testing done with the edge pump on and with it off.

Structure: The Vanishing Wall Often Stands on a Slope

Vanishing-edge pools are usually built where there is a view, which often means a hillside. Under California Building Code § 1808.7.3, any part of the pool wall within seven feet of the top of a slope must be able to hold the water in the pool without soil support. The vanishing wall is often that exact wall.

The structural questions are the same as in any hillside pool. Was the wall designed as freestanding? Was it built to the engineered detail, with the specified thickness, steel, and foundation? Cracking at the vanishing wall, a waterline that varies along the edge, or a weir that has gone out of level after it once ran evenly all point to movement and call for a structural investigation, not just a hydraulic adjustment.

Building the Record

The documents that decide these cases include the approved plans and the edge and basin details, the hydraulic calculations if any were prepared, the pump and plumbing specifications, the soils report and structural calculations, the permit and inspection record, and the owner's water bills and service history. Video of the edge running at design flow, recorded before anyone adjusts the equipment, is often the clearest evidence in the file. Under Code of Civil Procedure § 337.15, latent construction defect claims are subject to a ten-year limit measured from substantial completion, so it pays to gather these records early.
