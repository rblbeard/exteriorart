Pool Barriers, Drain Covers, and Drowning Litigation: What the Codes Actually Require

By Randy Beard · Swimming pool & construction defect expert witness · Updated November 7, 2026

Drowning cases are the hardest pool matters to try, and the ones where technical assumptions do the most damage. Counsel on both sides tend to arrive believing California requires a pool fence. The statute is narrower than that, the specifications are more particular, and the federal entrapment rules are a separate body of law applying to a different set of pools.

The stakes are not abstract. The CPSC's May 2026 submersion report counts an average of 376 pool- or spa-related fatal drownings per year among children under 15 for 2021 through 2023, and an estimated 5,900 emergency-department-treated nonfatal drownings per year for 2023 through 2025. Seventy-seven percent of the fatalities involved children under five; 71 percent occurred in residential settings, and 51 percent at the victim's own home. These are backyard cases.

As a swimming pool expert witness with 47 years in the industry — and a former investigator for the California Contractors State License Board — I am usually retained to answer a narrower question than liability: what did the code require of this pool on the date the permit issued, and what was actually built and maintained?

The Swimming Pool Safety Act Applies to Fewer Pools Than Counsel Expects

California's Swimming Pool Safety Act (Health & Safety Code §§ 115920–115929) is triggered by a building permit, not by ownership. Under § 115922, when a permit issues for construction of a new pool or spa — or for the remodel of an existing one — at a private single-family home, the pool must be equipped with at least two of seven listed drowning-prevention safety features. The local building official must inspect those features before granting final approval.

Two points matter constantly in litigation:

The count changed. SB 442 (Ch. 670, Stats. 2017) raised the requirement from one feature to two, effective January 1, 2018. A pool permitted in 2011 was never obligated to carry two. The applicable standard is fixed at the permit date, and I see that anachronism argued in both directions.

The exemptions are broad. Section 115925 excludes public swimming pools, and excludes apartment complexes and any residential setting other than a single-family home. It also exempts hot tubs and spas with locking safety covers meeting ASTM F1346. For an HOA, apartment, or hotel drowning, the Act is simply the wrong instrument — the analysis moves to the California Building Code's public-pool provisions, Title 22, local ordinance, and ordinary premises principles.

The seven features are specific and standard-referenced: a conforming isolation enclosure; removable mesh fencing meeting ASTM F2286 with a self-closing, self-latching gate; a safety pool cover meeting ASTM F1346; exit alarms on doors and windows with pool access; a self-closing, self-latching device released at least 54 inches above the floor on doors giving direct access; a water-entry alarm meeting ASTM F2208; or another means independently verified as equivalent by an approved testing laboratory.

The Barrier Specifications — and the Height Trap

Where an isolation enclosure is the chosen feature, § 115923 sets the dimensions — the measurements I take first on any site inspection:

  • Minimum height of 60 inches
  • Maximum vertical clearance from grade to the bottom of the enclosure of two inches
  • No gap or void permitting passage of a four-inch sphere
  • An outside surface free of protrusions, cavities, or other handholds for climbing
  • Gates that open away from the pool, self-closing, with a self-latching device no lower than 60 inches above the ground

Note three different latch heights in play. The residential enclosure gate latch sits at 60 inches. The device on a house door giving direct pool access — feature five — is specified at 54 inches. And under California Building Code § 3119B, governing public pool enclosures, hand-activated gate hardware must be no lower than 42 and no higher than 44 inches, because accessibility requirements control there. Applying the public-pool number to a backyard, or the backyard number to an apartment complex, is among the more common errors I am asked to correct in a competing expert's report.

Just as often the defect is not in the design but in what happened afterward: a gate that no longer closes the last two inches, a spring adjusted out to stop the slamming, a planter creating a handhold, grade eroded beneath a fence line, or a remodel that put a new door into the pool yard without a permit. A barrier must work every time, unassisted, and it degrades. Photographs and a tape measure at inspection are worth more than the permit file.

Entrapment Is a Separate Analysis Under Federal Law

Suction entrapment cases are governed by the Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act, effective December 19, 2008, which reaches public pools and spas — including hotel, apartment, and HOA facilities. Those pools must have drain covers compliant with the CPSC-incorporated successor standard, ANSI/APSP-16, and where the pool has a single main drain that is not unblockable, or multiple drains less than three feet apart, a second anti-entrapment system is required.

Two details decide many of these files. First, drain covers carry a manufacturer-marked useful life, commonly five to seven years; replacement past that date is best practice rather than a per-se VGB violation, but an expired or missing cover is a dated fact that is very difficult to explain away. Second, the design-side standard — ANSI/PHTA/ICC-7 — addresses the whole circulation system, not just the grate. Where the claim involves hair or limb entrapment, or a catch basin or vanishing-edge trough, the system-level analysis is where causation lives.

The Transfer Inspection Is Weaker Evidence Than It Appears

Business & Professions Code § 7195 requires that a home inspection performed when a single-family home with a pool changes hands identify which of the seven features are present, and report if there are fewer than two. It is frequently produced as proof of compliance. Read the limitation in the statute: the inspection is noninvasive, and it expressly does not require a determination whether a feature meets the ASTM or ASME specifications. A checked box confirms a cover exists. It does not establish that the cover meets F1346, that it was operable, or that the gate latched.
