NEC Article 680 in Pool and Spa Litigation: What Attorneys Need to Know

By Randy Beard · Swimming pool & construction defect expert witness · Updated November 7, 2026

When a pool or spa electrocution case lands on your desk, the controlling technical standard is almost always NEC Article 680 — the National Electrical Code's dedicated chapter governing electrical installations in and around swimming pools, spas, hot tubs, and fountains. Understanding how Article 680 works, which edition applied at the time of construction, and where the most common violations occur will help you evaluate claims faster and retain the right expert. As a swimming pool expert witness with 47 years in the industry, I have seen the same categories of electrical defects appear repeatedly in California construction-defect and wrongful-death litigation.

What NEC Article 680 Covers

Article 680 is one of the most detailed chapters in the National Electrical Code. It is organized by pool type:

  • Part II (Sections 680.20–680.29): Permanently installed pools — the section at issue in the vast majority of residential and HOA construction-defect cases.
  • Part IV (Sections 680.40–680.47): Spas and hot tubs.
  • Part V (Sections 680.50–680.57): Fountains and decorative water features.

The scope is broad: wiring, bonding, grounding, underwater luminaires, receptacles, overhead conductors, and equipment-room installations all fall within Article 680's reach. A deficiency in any one of these systems can create liability — especially when the resulting hazard is an energized pool or a swimmer exposed to electric shock drowning (ESD).

The Code-Edition Trap: Which NEC Applies?

California does not adopt the National Electrical Code in real time. The current standard is the 2025 California Electrical Code (CEC), based on the 2023 NEC, effective January 1, 2026. Pools permitted from January 1, 2023 through 2025 fall under the 2022 CEC (2020 NEC basis); from January 1, 2020 through 2022, the 2019 CEC (2017 NEC basis); and before that, the 2016 CEC (2014 NEC basis).

This matters enormously in litigation. The applicable code is the edition in effect when the building permit was issued — not the current code. A pool renovation also triggers compliance with the then-current code for the scope of work performed.

The 2008 NEC was a watershed for pool electrical safety. It added a mandatory GFCI requirement for single-phase pump motors (NEC 680.21), which did not exist under prior editions. A pool built in 2005 and renovated without permit in 2012 sits in a grey zone that an experienced swimming pool expert witness can help define.

Equipotential Bonding: The Most Litigated Provision

NEC Section 680.26 — equipotential bonding — is the provision most frequently at issue when swimmers experience shock or electric shock drowning. The purpose of equipotential bonding is not to carry fault current to earth; it is to eliminate voltage differences between all conductive surfaces a swimmer can simultaneously touch. A failed bonding system can produce sub-threshold AC current in the water — enough to paralyze a swimmer without obvious visible shock.

Key requirements under NEC 680.26:

  • Every metal component within 5 feet horizontally and 12 feet vertically of the inside pool wall must be bonded to a common bonding grid: structural reinforcing steel, ladders, rails, pump motors, filter housings, light fixture shells, and metal drain covers.
  • The pool water itself must be bonded, typically through a metal fitting, ladder, or listed water bonding device.
  • The bonding conductor minimum is #8 AWG solid copper.
  • For conductive pool shells without structural reinforcing steel, NEC 680.26(B)(1)(b) requires a #8 AWG copper conductor grid at 12-inch spacing. Separately, perimeter surfaces within 3 feet of the inside pool wall must be bonded under 680.26(B)(2), and that perimeter bonding is among the provisions most commonly omitted.

In my experience inspecting pool construction failures, omitted bonding grids, broken bonding continuity after replastering, and unbonded equipment added during renovation are the three most frequent bonding deficiencies. A single severed bonding connection after a remodel can re-energize what was previously a compliant pool.

GFCI Failures and Underwater Luminaires

Ground-fault circuit-interrupter (GFCI) protection is required at multiple points under NEC 680:

  • All receptacles within 20 feet of the inside pool wall must be GFCI-protected (NEC 680.22(A)(4)); no receptacle may be placed within 6 feet of the water.
  • Underwater luminaires operating above 15 volts must be GFCI-protected (NEC 680.23(A)(3)).
  • Pump motors — single-phase, 15A and 20A, 120V–240V — require GFCI protection (NEC 680.21(A)(1), added in the 2008 NEC).

Underwater light fixtures (Section 680.23) carry their own requirements beyond GFCI. Wet-niche luminaires must be installed a minimum of 18 inches below the normal water surface, must be listed for the application, and must use an identified transformer — not an autotransformer. Non-listed fixtures, improperly rated transformers, and fixtures installed above minimum depth are frequent construction-defect findings. Deteriorated conduit or fitting seals at the forming shell — allowing water infiltration into the wiring — are another recurring issue that I identify during site inspection.

Overhead Conductors and Wiring Method Violations

NEC Section 680.8 prohibits overhead conductors over pool water and sets clearance minimums: 22.5 feet above maximum water level for most open wiring. This is often violated when a pool is installed near an existing service drop — a scenario common in older California neighborhoods where lateral additions push pools toward property lines and utility easements.

On wiring method, Article 680 requires rigid metal conduit (RMC), intermediate metal conduit (IMC), or Schedule 80 PVC within 5 feet of the pool and below grade. The use of NM cable (Romex) in pool-area wiring is a clear NEC violation and commonly surfaces in cases involving unlicensed contractor work or unpermitted construction.

Building the Expert Framework for Electrical Cases

Pool electrical litigation typically requires at least two expert disciplines: a licensed electrical engineer or master electrician to address NEC compliance, and a pool construction expert who can speak to whether the deficiencies were the result of faulty installation, deferred maintenance, or unpermitted renovation. My C-27 landscape and irrigation and C-53 swimming pool contractor licenses, combined with my years as an investigator for the California Contractors State License Board, position me to address both the construction standard of care and the regulatory context in which the work was performed.

A thorough site inspection, coupled with code-edition analysis tied to the permit history, will establish whether a violation was a construction defect at the time of installation or a subsequent maintenance failure — a distinction that drives both liability allocation and damages.
