Pool Plaster Delamination and Surface Defects: What Attorneys Need to Know

By Randy Beard · Swimming pool & construction defect expert witness · Updated November 7, 2026

Few construction-defect claims generate more finger-pointing than a failed pool interior finish. The homeowner sees flaking and rough patches within a year of a five-figure replaster. The plastering contractor blames the service company's water chemistry; the service company blames the plaster mix.

The good news for counsel is that plaster failures are among the more diagnosable defects in residential construction: the evidence survives, and the competing explanations leave different signatures. As a swimming pool expert witness with 47 years in the industry, I find most of these cases turn on a few technical distinctions that get blurred in the pleadings.

The Vocabulary Matters: Debonding, Delamination, and Spalling

These three terms are used interchangeably in demand letters and almost never mean the same thing to a qualified expert. The distinction drives causation.

  • Debonding is an adhesive failure — the plaster coat separates from the substrate beneath it, whether the gunite shell or an older layer of plaster left in place during a resurface.
  • Delamination is a cohesive failure — the finish coat splits within itself, coming apart in a layer rather than releasing from what is underneath.
  • Spalling is shallow surface loss, typically a sixteenth to an eighth of an inch, flaking away in small crater-like or pitted areas.

These modes point in different directions. Debonding raises questions about surface preparation — bond failure over fresh gunite is uncommon, while bonding new plaster to old plaster is where the industry sees most adhesive failures. Delamination and spalling point inward, at the mix and the application itself.

The Application Window Is Where Most Defects Are Created

Plaster is a cementitious material, unforgiving of shortcuts in the few hours it is placed and finished. Three variables recur in the cases I inspect:

Water-to-cement ratio. Excess water in the mix weakens the finished product and makes it prone to shrinkage and bond failure — the single most consequential decision made at the job site.

Troweling with water on the surface. When a crew trowels while free water is still present, that water is forced back into the paste, creating a weak, porous skin over otherwise sound plaster. This is a classic precursor to spalling, and it explains why spalling appears in predictable places — step radiuses and shallow-end floors, where crews overwork the finish.

Calcium chloride set accelerant. Used to speed set times in cool conditions, excess accelerant contributes to shrinkage and micro-cracking, and is associated with gray mottling in white plaster.

Each leaves evidence. Mapping the failures against the pool's geometry often separates an application defect from a service-related one: application defects cluster where the crew worked hardest or where a batch changed, not uniformly across every wetted surface.

The Water-Chemistry Defense — and Where It Runs Out

The most common defense in a plaster case is that aggressive water dissolved the finish. It is a legitimate mechanism, measured by the Langelier Saturation Index (LSI), which expresses how saturated water is with calcium carbonate. Perfect saturation is 0.00, and the accepted operating range is -0.3 to +0.3. Water below that range pulls calcium from the most available source — the cement in the plaster or pebble finish — producing etching. Water above it precipitates scale. Both can permanently damage a surface.

But the defense has limits, and they are testable:

  • Etching is a chemistry pattern, not a geometry pattern. Aggressive water contacts every wetted surface. When damage is confined to steps, benches, or a single wall, chemistry is a poor explanation.
  • Spalling forms before the pool is filled. That weakness is created during troweling. Aggressive water can accelerate the loss of an already-compromised surface, but it did not create it.
  • Startup matters enormously. Roughly 86 percent of the cement in new plaster hydrates within the first 28 days, and the National Plasterers Council's startup protocols address that window specifically. What happened in the first month is often more probative than a year of later service records.

Calcium Nodules and Craze Cracking as Diagnostic Evidence

Two visible conditions are especially useful, because each reveals a hidden problem.

Craze cracking is a fine network of surface shrinkage cracks, commonly resulting from an overly wet mix, excessive set accelerant, adding water during troweling, or letting the plaster dry out before the pool is filled. Crazing also opens pathways for staining and harbors black algae.

Calcium nodules are small volcano-shaped deposits of calcium carbonate, often sitting on a fine crack and trailing down a wall like an icicle. They form where water has penetrated behind or into the plaster — through a delamination or severe crazing — dissolved calcium hydroxide, and carried it back to the surface to harden. Nodules are therefore not a cosmetic complaint. They are a surface expression of a subsurface bond problem, and they strengthen a defect theory over a maintenance one.

Standards, Timing, and Building the Record

The trade's reference document is the National Plasterers Council Technical Manual, now in its 10th edition, recognized by organizations including ACI, ICC, and ANSI. For the pool itself, ANSI/APSP/ICC-5 governs design and construction criteria.

On timing, latent defects are subject to the ten-year outside limit under Code of Civil Procedure section 337.15, measured from substantial completion; patent defects fall under the four-year period in section 337.1. The California Supreme Court has held that the ten-year period is not equitably tolled while repair promises remain pending — a trap in plaster disputes, where contractors often make successive patch attempts over several seasons. Where the pool was part of new residential construction sold after January 1, 2003, the Right to Repair Act (SB 800, Civil Code section 895 et seq.) may impose its own component-specific periods and pre-litigation procedures.

The most valuable early step is a site inspection before any remediation. Once the pool is drained, chipped, and replastered, the evidence is gone. Photograph the failures in place and preserve core samples for petrographic analysis. Secure the permit history, the plastering subcontract and mix specifications, the startup instruction sheet, and the service and water-test logs — whether a startup sheet was provided, and followed, frequently determines allocation.
