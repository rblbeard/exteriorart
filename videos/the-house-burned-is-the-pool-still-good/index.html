The House Burned. Is the Pool Still Good?

Video by Randy Beard · Construction Defects & Forensic Analysis · 5:28 min · Published 2026-10-01

What this video covers

A pool full of water acts as a heat sink, so after a wildfire the shell below the waterline often survives while the bond beam, coping, tile and deck take the heat. What to look at, and what testing tells you whether the pool can be saved.

When a wildfire tears through a neighborhood, it leaves almost nothing behind. Homes are leveled. Trees are reduced to charred spikes. But amidst this total devastation, you will often find a bizarre survivor. An intact swimming pool, still holding water. Seeing a pool full of water might make you think it escaped the inferno completely unscathed. But water holding its shape does not mean the concrete holding the water is structurally found. To accurately assess the damage, we have to stop looking at the pool as a single, solid block of concrete. Instead, we need to view it as an insulated vessel. This vessel interacts with the fire in three completely different ways.

Measuring the true toll of a wildfire requires us to break this vessel apart and perform a precise, zone-by-zone, thermal autopsy. Let's start with Zone 1, the coping. This exposed top edge of the pool is the front line. It sits entirely above ground and takes the absolute maximum thermal assault the fire can deliver. Concrete here faces its first critical failure point at exactly 572 degrees Fahrenheit. At this temperature, the iron compounds inside the cement begin to oxidize. Once exposed concrete reaches these extreme temperatures, its structural integrity is destroyed. It might look intact, but the material is effectively dead.

Which raises an immediate question. If the exposed coping fractured at 1000 degrees, why didn't the submerged floor suffer the exact same feat? The answer lies in the pool's massive water volume, acting as a thermodynamic heat sink. Tens of thousands of gallons of water can rapidly absorb and dispose an enormous amount of incoming thermal energy. And liquid water has a hard physical tap. Thermodynamics dictate it cannot exceed 212 degrees Fahrenheit, no matter how hot the air burns above it. This unbreakable ceiling is a physical guarantee. In fire ecology, we look at the physical composition of soil. Because the dirt is highly porous and filled with air and moisture, it is a phenomenally poor conductor of heat.
