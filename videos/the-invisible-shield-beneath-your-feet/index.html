The Invisible Shield Beneath Your Feet

Video by Randy Beard · Code Compliance — Title 24 & NEC Article 680 · 5:17 min · Published 2026-09-17

What this video covers

How the equipotential bonding grid beneath pool decks protects swimmers from electrical hazards — and exactly what inspectors look for under NEC Article 680.

It's a perfect summer afternoon. You're swimming in a pool surrounded by clear water, with underwater lights illuminating the deep end. But think about what's actually happening here. You're floating in a giant tub of water, surrounded by high-voltage electric pumps, pool heaters, and lighting systems. How is it that we can swim right next to all this electricity without getting shocked? To understand that, we have to look at how electricity behaves. Think of electricity like water on a hill. It only flows when it has a clear path from a high place to a low place. In electrical terms, that difference in height is called voltage. If everything around you is at the exact same electrical height, the electricity has nowhere to fall.

Without a lower place to flow toward, the kind has absolutely no reason to travel through your body. This brings us to a hidden hazard known as step potential. Step potential happens when you stand with your feet on two surfaces that have different electrical voltage levels. Your body becomes the bridge between high and low, turning you into a human wire. Watch what happens if we alter the ground itself. If we engineer the surface to sit at the exact same electrical level, that gradient vanishes. We call this state of perfect electrical balance equipotential bonding. Equipotential bonding is an engineered safety system that, connects all the metal parts and walking surfaces around a pool, so they share the exact same voltage level.

Because of this engineered equalized zone, a wet barefoot swimmer doesn't become a human lightning rod. There is simply no difference in voltage to push the current through them. To see how this balance is created, we have to look underground. The foundation of this system is a solid number eight copper wire. The most critical vulnerability in this system lies in the three-flip perimeter of walking surface immediately surrounding the water's edge. Wet concrete and masonry pavers are highly conductive. Any stray electrical current from a faulty pump or a nearby lightning strike can easily travel right through these wet deck materials. If we fail to protect this specific three-foot perimeter, the water itself might be safe, but simply stepping out of the pool could become a lethal mistake.

The National Electrical Code requires a specific solution for that three-foot perimeter. This 12-inch by 12-inch copper grid is laid beneath the deck surface. With this tight mesh, a swimmer's foot is always inches from bonded copper, completely neutralizing step potentials across the pavers. Safety Update TIA 23-9 clarifies that depth is everything. The Code requires the grid to be secured strictly between 4 and 6 inches below the finished grade. This shallow placement drastically improves its ability to equalize voltage under bare feet. Getting this measurement right is critical. The exact depth of that copper mesh is the difference between a highly functional, life-saving shield and a completely useless piece of buried wire.

Burying nettle in the ground comes with its own set of problems. Over time, soil soaked with groundwater, fertilizers, and pool chemicals is highly corrosive. If a builder uses unlisted or improper wire mesh, it can rust and dissolve away, quietly weaving the entire pool unprotected. Because of this, an upcoming mandate in the 2025 National Electrical Code will require all unencapsulated grids to be formally listed for corrosion resistance and mechanical performance. Listed means the equipment has been independently tested and certified by an approved safety organization. This new rule forces manufacturers to prove that their copper and steel grids can survive decades of being buried in wet, chemical-heavy dirt without losing their electrical continuity.

The Electrical Code keeps the pool safe for your swim today. More importantly, these material standards guarantee that this invisible safety net holds strome for decades to come. Let's look back above ground. This image shows the idyllic pool setting, or at least a scene, full of people relaxing by the water. When you see a family walking barefoot across those wet pavers, the lack of electrical hazard is a testament to brilliant engineering. The deck around the pool serves a highly specific technical purpose. It is an actively engineered electrical safe zone, disguised as a patio for your lounge chairs. Just four to six inches below the finished grade, a hidden web of copper is working silently, managing the electrical forces of nature so we don't have to.

True public safety is rarely something you can spot with the naked eye. It relies on the rigorous, invisible standards, built right beneath our feet, that allow us to step out of the water and live without fear of a shock.
