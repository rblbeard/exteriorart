Pool Heaters in 2026 — The Title 24 Contractor’s Guide

What this video covers

California’s pool-heating rules changed on December 31, 2025. What changed, which projects are captured, what plan checkers are flagging, and how to build a submittal that clears the counter the first time.

Transcript

If you make your living digging, framing, and finishing pools in California, your standard operating procedure expires on December 31st, 2025. When the clock hits midnight, the rules for how you heap out water change completely. The state is activating the 2025 update to its Title 24 building energy efficiency standards. This aligns with California's broader decarbonization mandate, a legislative requirement designed to transition the state away from fossil fuels. The core restriction is simple but severe. You can no longer install natural gas or electric resistance heaters as the primary heat source for a new swimming pool. The California Energy Commission drafted this binding legal requirement, empowering local building departments to reject any permit application that fails to meet these new standards.

For decades, the industry standard was straightforward. Drop a 400,000 BTU gas heater onto a pad, wire it up, and move to the next job. Now, that exact installation will fail inspection. The era of slapping a massive standalone gas heater on a newly dug pool is over. To survive, contractors have to evolve from fast-paced installers into complex energy advisors, guiding clients through highly restricted equipment choices. Right now, there is a rush of homeowners trying to secure their permits before 2026, driven by rumors of a total gas ban. But those rumors miss a crucial legal detail. The code draws a strict line between new construction, which includes adding heat to a previously unheated pool, and an equipment repair.

Under Section 110.4, replacing a broken heater on an existing pool is a repair. New rules don't apply. Like-for-like gas swaps are allowed. But if digging a new pool or adding heat to an unheated pool, Title 24 triggers. Primary gas heating is banned. So you can still profitably service your existing customer base with simple gas heater replacement. But the moment you start digging a new hole in the ground, your bids and your system designs have to radically change. Once a project triggers Title 24, you need a compliant primary heat source. The most common solution is the heat pump pool here. Unlike traditional units that burn fuel to create heat, heat pumps operate on a refrigeration cycle, pulling ambient warmth out of the surrounding air and transferring it directly into the pool wire.

The coefficient of performance, or COP, is a metric measuring how much useful heat a system puts out compared to the electrical energy it consumes. Thanks to this cycle, heat pumps achieve efficiency ratings of 300% to 700%. This chart illustrates the critical vulnerability of heat pumps. Their performance is directly tied to the ambient air temperature. While the efficiency curve is excellent at 80 degrees, it drops drastically as the air temperature approaches 40 degrees.

And then there is the raw output problem. The average residential heat pump maxes out at about 140,000 BTUs of heating capacity, which is a fraction of the power delivered by a standard 400,000 BTU gas heater. That means a heat pump would drastically lower a homeowner's monthly utility bill, but it only heats the water by 1 to 1.5 degrees per hour. You have to retrain your clients to maintain steady baseline temperatures instead of turning the heater on at the last minute. The second primary compliance option under the code is a solar thermal system. To rely on solar thermal, you must account for the massive scale of the installation required to soak up enough intense California sunlight. This scale is necessary to heat thousands of gallons of pool wire. The state energy code enforces a strict non-negotiable size requirement. For a single family home, the solar away must equal at least 60% of the pool's total surface area. This creates immediate geographic and weather constraints. The system acquires extensive, unshaded roof space, and it offers absolutely zero heating capability at night, or during overcast winter weeks.

Solar thermal provides the fastest long-term return on investment, because the heat itself is free. However, that strict 60% surface area requirement makes it physically impossible to install on many small or heavily shaded residential lots. Both of these options run directly into the Friday night spa door. The solar thermal system is designed to provide the best possible heating service to those who are having a busy and busy night spa dilemma. A luxury homeowner expects to hit a button and watch their spa heat from 70 degrees to 102 degrees in 30 minutes. Stand-alone heat pumps and solar thermal systems lack the raw thermal output required to meet the specific rapid heating demand due to their low BTU ratings. To solve this, Title 24 provides a hybrid system pathway.

This allows you to pair a primary renewable source, like the heat pump, with a secondary gas heater. The code also mandates friction-reducing sweep elbows, properly sized pumps, and smart valves walking out the gas heater. So gas survives as a lunchery spa accelerator. But restricting it to a secondary role means contractors have to design complex, smart-controlled plumbing systems that require significantly higher upfront capital costs. Knowing the code is only half the job. The other half is translating these technical restrictions into a practical sales framework that matches the right system to the right buyer. If you have a budget-kudges client building a standalone pool in a consistently warm climate, the heat pump is the ideal match. But you have to set clear expectations upfront.

You must explain that they are trading spontaneous, rapid heating for high efficiency, meaning they need to plan ahead if they want a warm pool on Saturday morning. Alternatively, the solar thermal system is the perfect fit for the forever home. If a buyer plans to stay on the property for a decade or more, they have the time required to fully recoup their investment. By framing the high upfront cost of a heat pump or a massive solar array as prepaying for a decade of utility bills, you can turn a regulatory hurdle into a compelling financial selling point. Then there is the high-end buyer. This is the client paying for a custom gunite pool with a premium attached spa, and they demand high performance no matter the weather.

For this buyer, you must pitch the hybrid system. It is the only legally viable solution that validates their need for rapid, aggressive heating while keeping the entire project fully code compliant. You will need to present the financial reality clearly. Engineering this dual system setup will cause a 10-15% increase in total project costs. Standardizing on this hybrid upgrade protects your business from angry winter callbacks when a standalone unit fails to reach temperature in 40 degree weather. This transition is not a passing trend. It reflects the immense legislative momentum and administrative authority driving California toward total building electrification.
