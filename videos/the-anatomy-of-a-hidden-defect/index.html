The Anatomy of a Hidden Defect

Video by Randy Beard · Construction Defects & Forensic Analysis · 5:03 min · Published 2026-08-22

What this video covers

When a pool is re-plastered, the demolition method quietly decides how long the new finish lives. Impact tools drive interconnected micro-cracks — “bruising” — into the shell. You can’t see it, tapping can’t find it, and the new finish bonds to it anyway.

Transcript

It sounds like progress. A crew descends into an empty swimming pool, fires up heavy demolition hammers, and aggressively strips away the old failing plaster. But this fast-paced teardown is actually the exact moment the pool's structural future begins to die. To understand why, you have to look at what a pool shell actually is. It's an incredibly thin structural layer, typically just 5 to 8 inches of concrete. It fights a two-front war, holding back tons of water from the inside and tons of shifting earth from the outside. Older pools have practically zero excess strength to spare. The smooth plaster surface everyone thinks of as the pool isn't structural at all. It's merely a waterproof veneer bonded to that highly stressed shell. After a crew hammers off that old veneer, they usually perform a diagnostic test called sounding. They tap the exposed shell with a hand tool, listening for hull-less spots. If they hear a solid thud, they assume the shell is healthy, ready for a new coat. There is a fatal flaw in this test. The American Concrete Institute specifically warns that sounding is completely incapable of detecting microscopic impact damage. Because the crew cannot hear or see the fractures they just inflicted, they are about to spread a brand new plaster over a broken foundation. Structural engineers call this hidden damage bruising. The official guidelines define a bruised surface as a weak layer of interconnected microcracks, directly caused by the blunt force of pneumatic hammers. This is an optical microscope view of concrete core cross-sections. You can see exactly how the kinetic energy from a demolition breaker shatters the shotcrete below the surface, leaving a dense web of microscopic cracks.

Microscopic damage creates a deep structural vulnerability. X-ray CT scan research reveals these impact fractures penetrate over an inch and a half into the concrete base. Inside that bruised zone, testing shows concrete cores pulled from a chipped substrate retain only 40% of their original structural strength. Every pass of an impact hammer compromises nearly a third of the shell's remaining structural integrity while it shaves away the plaster. This loss of strength is a serious problem. A pool renovation relies entirely on a mechanical bond. When you apply fresh plaster to a fully cured decades-old pool shell, you get zero chemical adhesion. The new material has to physically grip the rough surface of the old concrete. If that new plaster physically grips a layer of bruised, crumbling concrete, the weakest link in the entire system becomes the broken concrete itself. We can quantify this failure using field data from highway departments that repair bridge decks. On a chart comparing bond strengths, we can see that jackhammered surfaces pull off at 80 PSI, missing the 100 PSI minimum. But hydrodemolition surfaces see free pass at 121 to 166 PSI, sometimes reaching 300 PSI. Those catastrophic delamination callbacks that pool contractors dread, where large sheets of new finish peel away, are the inevitable result of a bruised substrate failing from the inside out.

Beyond protecting the substrate from microcracking, water blasting preserves the full structural effectiveness of the pool, eliminates dangerous silica dust in the air, and leaves embedded steel rebar completely clean and uncompromised. It also creates an ideal open-pore, high-retextured profile. This crater-like surface guarantees the maximum mechanical grip for the fresh finish. Looking at this timeline graphic based on data from the Swedish Cement and Concrete Research Institute, the real-world results of that grip are undeniable. A repair relying on mechanical chiseling fails in 7 to 12 years. A hydro demolition repair survives 21 to 35 years.

By choosing high-pressure water over a heavy hammer, you extract roughly three times the lifespan out of the exact same pool. Before you approve any renovation on your property, ask your contractor a short chain of questions. What specific removal tools do you use? If you use hammers, what size are they? And do you follow up with secondary remedial blasting and verifiable bond testing? If they can't give you a straight answer, find a different crew. The method used to strip a pool is the single deciding factor in whether your structure survives for another generation.
